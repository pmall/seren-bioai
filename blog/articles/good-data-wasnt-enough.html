Protein to Pill

Good data wasn't enough

9 October 2026

Caribou Biosciences, co-founded by Jennifer Doudna, halted all research and development this week and put itself up for sale, shelving two allogeneic CAR-T programs with response rates most biotechs would frame as validation. Its lead, vispa-cel, an off-the-shelf CD19 CAR-T, had posted 82% overall response and 67% complete response with no graft-versus-host disease or severe immune-effector toxicity; the BCMA follow-up CB-011 had posted 92% overall response, also with no graft-versus-host disease. The company ended June with $113.8 million in cash. None of it mattered. Management said it was "impossible to secure capital" for allogeneic cell therapy, and a peer chief executive called the funding climate a "nuclear winter".

The kill was financial, not biological

That distinction is the story. The autologous autoimmune CAR-T pause last month — Novartis suspending eight trials after three deaths, BMS pausing its own program after inflammatory events — was at least arguable on safety, and the subsequent construct-level split gave the field something to learn: Kyverna's fully-human CD28 construct holding one-year durability with no high-grade events against rapid-manufacturing platforms that ran into trouble. Caribou offers no such lesson. Its programs were cut with clean graft-versus-host sheets and registrational designations in hand, backed historically by Novartis, AbbVie, and Pfizer money. The buyer pool, not the biology, walked away — with capital rotating toward autologous and in vivo approaches and leaving the allogeneic shelf empty.

A selective thaw, not a recovery

The broader capital picture sharpens the point rather than softening it. Third-quarter layoffs were the lowest since tracking began — 27 companies and 2,568 affected staff, down 56% and 90% year over year, which reads as stabilization until you look at where the money actually went. The quarter's five largest venture raises totaled about $1.5 billion and concentrated in derisked or AI-named assets — Chai's $400M Series C, Enveda's $311M Series E, and three late-stage platform bets. At the other end, two Massachusetts biotechs simply closed in September for lack of clinical-data fundraising, and September's market moves punished the single-asset binary while rewarding maintenance-of-effect: Viking holding 17.7% weight loss off drug and raising $575M against Longeveron falling 60% on a Phase 2b miss with about $10M left to the fourth quarter.

Caribou sits exactly in the gap those numbers describe: good Phase 1 data in an out-of-favor modality, with no path to pay for the Phase 2 that would prove it. The allogeneic thesis — one donor lot treating many patients, at cell-therapy potency without per-patient manufacturing — was always a financing thesis as much as a biology thesis, because the clinical proof takes more lots, more patients, and more years than an autologous program. When the funding window narrows to derisked assets, the modality with the longest bridge to revenue dies first, however clean its early look.

What to watch

First, whether anything bids — a merger or asset sale that keeps vispa-cel alive inside an autologous or in vivo buyer would confirm the programs have value and only the standalone vehicle failed. Second, whether the in vivo rotation absorbs the logic Caribou was built on: off-the-shelf B-cell depletion without cell manufacturing is exactly what mRNA-encoded engagers now promise, and Novartis buying that alternative weeks after pausing its own cell-therapy trials suggests the demand didn't vanish, it moved format. Third, how many more positive Phase 1s get shelved this way before the venture bar moves — the layoff trough says the industry stopped bleeding; Caribou says it hasn't started betting again.
