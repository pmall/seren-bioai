Protein to Pill

The state builds the trial machine

2 October 2026

The US government launched a set of AI-driven programs to overhaul how clinical trials are designed and run — not a new drug, but a new way of proving drugs work. The timing matters: China passed the US on trial counts six years ago, and the American answer is now framed explicitly as trial infrastructure as software.

What SURPASS actually funds

SURPASS, the ARPA-H program at the center, has three engines: a phaseless design engine using digital twins and pre-launch simulation, a continuous inference engine meant to shrink conventional control arms, and an agentic operations layer for startup, arm onboarding, and data cleaning. Around it sit three infrastructure pieces: STACK for AI-assisted site activation including research-naïve sites, COMMONS for a national consent architecture, and CINCH for patient real-world-data navigation.

The vocabulary is worth pausing on. Phaseless means a trial that does not march 1-2-3 but adapts as evidence accumulates; digital twins mean simulated patients used to borrow strength from history so fewer real patients need to sit in a control arm; agentic ops means software that does the startup paperwork, cleans the data, and onboards new arms without waiting on a contract research organization queue. None of this is proven at national scale. All of it targets the part of drug development where most of the time and money actually goes.

Why the state, why now

This is the second move in a sequence, not the first. It follows the Operation TrialBlazer IND pilot, open through October 30, plus the FDA's Elsa rollout and a 2,200-hire rebuild after more than 3,000 cuts. Read together, the pattern is agency throughput as well as trial-design throughput: faster to open a trial, faster to run it, faster to review it.

The competitive framing is explicit — the US risks losing clinical-trial leadership — and the geography behind it is real. Some 82% of 140 global in vivo CAR-T programs were developed in China and moved into people through the hospital-led IIT fast path, and Beijing's five-year plan aims to grow the industry to 3.5 trillion yuan with a quarter of global first-in-class drugs originating there. China takes discovery risk in its hospital system and exports the evidence. The SURPASS answer is to make the American system — sites, consent, patient data — equally programmable.

The clinical use case phaseless designs were made for

The same week, pancreatic cancer offered a preview of what such machinery would actually run. After the August approval of daraxonrasib, researchers pushed an interception strategy: the PRECEDE high-risk cohort has enrolled 12,000 of a planned 20,000 people and already found more than 50 mostly early cancers, with a stated goal of lifting five-year survival from 13.7% toward 50% through surveillance plus adjuvant KRAS-directed drugs. Supporting pieces include AI-assisted scan reading, blood-test evaluation, and mouse work showing Kras-precancer interception improves survival.

Interception breaks the phased-trial mold by construction. You surveil healthy-but-high-risk people for years, act on an early signal, then treat adjuvantly with a targeted drug — a continuous loop of detection and intervention, not a three-act trial. That is exactly the shape phaseless designs with digital twins and continuous inference claim to handle: borrowing control information across time, simulating arms before launching them, adding cohorts without restarting the machine. Whether SURPASS can run that loop at national scale is unproven. That it names the right loop is new.

What to watch

This extends a shift this blog has tracked from industry — from models that generate molecules to systems that prove they work — into the state layer. Companies already sell trial design, patient matching, and agentic chemistry as infrastructure; now the funder of last resort wants the consent, site, and data rails underneath. The checks are concrete: whether TrialBlazer graduates into standing process after October 30, whether STACK actually activates research-naïve sites or just certifies the usual ones faster, whether COMMONS produces reusable consent or another pilot portal, and whether a phaseless design with a shrunken control arm survives its first FDA review. Until then, the week's lesson is plain: the bottleneck moved past the molecule, and the state noticed.
