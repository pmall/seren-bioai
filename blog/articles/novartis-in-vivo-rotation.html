Protein to Pill

The in vivo rotation gets a price tag

3 October 2026

Novartis will pay up to $7.8 billion for an mRNA-encoded CD3xCD19 T cell engager from China's Abogen — $575 million upfront plus milestones and royalties, with an exclusive option on further RNA-platform therapies. The same buyer that suspended eight autoimmune CAR-T trials after three deaths weeks earlier just bought the in vivo alternative to the same biology, off the shelf.

What ABO2203 actually is

ABO2203 is not a cell product. It is an mRNA that instructs the body to produce a CD3xCD19 bispecific T cell engager inside the patient — B cell depletion without apheresis, lymphodepletion, or a cell-manufacturing slot. Indications are undisclosed, with autoimmune potential framed around the deal.

The human data are tiny: a first-in-human in relapsed/refractory B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma in nine patients, with zero cytokine release syndrome, zero dose-limiting toxicity, and zero neuro events claimed, and 100% response at the top dose. Nine patients cannot prove that an in vivo TCE avoids the immune-effector toxicities that sank its ex vivo cousin. It can only explain why Novartis paid for the option to find out.

Why the rotation, why now

The ex vivo autoimmune experiment hit its safety reckoning in September, when Novartis suspended eight autoimmune and neurology trials of rapid-manufacturing CAR-T rap-cel after three patient deaths from immune effector cell-associated hemophagocytic syndrome, and BMS paused its own autoimmune program after inflammatory events. The field had imported the oncology safety profile into a non-oncology population with different immune baselines, and the import failed.

The industry response since has been consistent: ArsenalBio halted all ex vivo CAR-T for solid tumors to go entirely in vivo, TScan cut three-quarters of staff to pivot to in vivo TCR-T, and some 82% of 140 global in vivo programs turned out to have been developed in China and moved into people through the hospital-led investigator-initiated trial fast path. Discovery risk taken in one system, evidence imported into another. Novartis buying ABO2203 extends that logic one step beyond CAR-T: in vivo logic now covers T cell engagers too.

One supply line, four modalities

This is Novartis's third China-originated deal this year, after a $900 million radioligand pact with BoomRay and a $1.5 billion neuroscience deal with SciNeuro. Zoom out a week and the pattern is industry-wide: Merck paid up to $2.13 billion for a preclinical KRAS glue from SciBrunch, Novo paid up to $2.6 billion for Hengrui's weekly oral dual incretin, and AstraZeneca took a $2 billion strategic stake in Summit to fund the Akeso-originated PD-1/VEGF bispecific ivonescimab without buying a license at all.

TCEs, KRAS glues, oral incretins, PD-1/VEGF bispecifics — different modalities, same geography. The paperwork varies (upfront plus milestones, equity without license, platform option), because the supply line is being invented as fast as it is being used.

What to watch

Three questions decide whether $7.8 billion was rotation or retreat. First, does zero CRS survive scale — nine lymphoma patients into larger B-NHL cohorts, then into autoimmune patients whose cytokine baselines differ. Second, does the durability that justifies chronic B cell depletion hold when the drug is a transient mRNA rather than a persisting cell. Third, what the platform option actually covers: a single-asset hedge against the rap-cel write-off, or a standing RNA-encoded antibody foundry inside Novartis.

Until then, the week's lesson is the one this blog has tracked since the pauses: safety is partitioning by construct and setting, not by target. CD19 still works. The cell around it was the problem.
