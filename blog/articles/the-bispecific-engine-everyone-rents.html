Protein to Pill

The bispecific engine everyone rents

26 September 2026

Four platform deals in one week, one business model

Genentech agreed to pay Earendil Labs more than $1.5 billion in total, starting with $55 million upfront, for AI-designed cancer bispecifics: Earendil leads discovery and early research on pre-agreed target combinations, Genentech takes global development and commercialization. The targets are undisclosed.

It did not land alone. The same day, Roche committed up to $2 billion ($70 million upfront) to Atavistik for allosteric small-molecule cardiometabolic programs, while Lilly set aside up to $3.35 billion ($100 million upfront, five targets) with InnoCare for discovery across cancer and autoimmunity. Days earlier Roche had bet up to $1 billion ($36.5 million upfront) on Dualitas, whose DualScreen platform screens more than 300,000 bispecific combinations, discovering target pairs from the biology rather than pre-specifying them.

A bispecific antibody grabs two targets at once — two tumor antigens, or a tumor antigen plus a T-cell recruiter — which makes the design space combinatorial and the manufacturing finicky. That is exactly the kind of search problem AI discovery platforms claim to be good at, and pharma is now buying the search rather than building it.

Earendil joins Chai as shared infrastructure

Earendil is becoming a supplier to competing pharmas, not a captive arm of one. It raised $787 million privately in March, then signed Sanofi in April 2025 ($125 million upfront, up to $1.72 billion for two autoimmune and bowel-disease bispecifics) before adding Genentech for oncology. Sanofi plus Genentech on the same engine mirrors the Chai pattern: Chai-2 posts roughly 20% zero-shot antibody hit rates — about 100-fold above prior computational methods — and the same models are now licensed non-exclusively to Novartis, Pfizer, Lilly, BMS, and argenx.

Roche's side of the ledger tells the same story from the buyer's seat. It already sells bispecifics — Vabysmo, Lunsumio, Columvi — and this month stacked discovery supply underneath the franchise: Dualitas for immunology bispecifics, Atavistik for cardiometabolic allosterics, on top of earlier Hanmi, DualityBio, and Nurix pacts described alongside the Atavistik deal. Lilly, for its part, extended a 2026 deal spree past $25 billion with the InnoCare pact as reported with the announcement.

The pricing says the quiet part out loud. Upfronts of $36.5 to $100 million with billion-plus milestone tails is what you pay for an option on someone else's discovery throughput — not an acquisition, not an exclusive. Discovery AI is being priced as shared infrastructure, the way cloud compute or contract manufacturing is priced.

Why this week matters

Bispecifics were supposed to be a proprietary edge: whoever figured out the pairing rules and the CMC would own a franchise. This week suggests the opposite equilibrium — everybody rents the same pairing engines, and differentiation moves downstream to target choice, trial execution, and commercial reach.

That is new for a field that spent the last two years arguing about whose model had the best hit rate. Chai's answer was that the model could be shared and the data flywheel kept. Earendil's week generalizes it: two big pharmas, two therapy areas, one supplier. If the molecules work, the moat was never the generator — it was the throughput of deciding which combinations to make and the discipline to kill the rest.

What to watch

Three things decide whether shared infrastructure is a good deal or a crowded trade. First, whether non-exclusive engines produce exclusive drugs — if Sanofi's and Genentech's bispecifics come from the same discovery logic, the clinic has to separate what the platform cannot. Second, whether screening scale (300,000 combinations at Dualitas, full-length antibodies at 20% hits at Chai) converts into developable molecules rather than interesting binders. Third, whether the upfront-plus-milestones structure survives a failure: $55–100 million options are cheap until three of them miss, at which point someone asks what the platform actually knew.

If the candidates advance, the template sticks: pharma stops pretending discovery platforms are acquisitions-in-waiting and treats them as utilities. The engine gets rented. The drugs, if any, stay proprietary.
