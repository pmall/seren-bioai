Protein to Pill

Paying for the missing data

16 September 2026

Within about twenty-four hours, four different actors attacked the same problem from four different sides: there is not enough good biology data to train on, so someone has to pay to create it, pool it, or give it away. The OpenAI Foundation launched Data for Public Health with $40M for UNC cancer-vaccine data collection, Enveda revived the CASMI small-molecule identification contest as a $50k Kaggle competition feeding its PRISM foundation model, Therna Biosciences open-released Chronos, the mRNA measurement engine behind its RNA biologist, and Mithrl raised a $20M Series A to sell grounded biomedical AI infrastructure to pharma. The scarce input was never the model. This week it got buyers.

A foundation starts buying datasets

The most unusual entrant is not a biotech at all. The OpenAI Foundation — which holds 26% of OpenAI equity and aims at $1B in grants by year-end — made biology data creation its second science program, with the explicit thesis that data is the biggest bottleneck. The money so far: $40M to UNC, support for OpenAdmet prediction competitions, and $500k to 1Day Sooner for a "biotech's lost archive" of bankrupt companies' regulatory documents for AI copilots.

The UNC detail matters: hundreds of de-identified tumor and immune samples from three biobanks to train antigen-selection AI, with parallel comparisons of vaccine formulations in triple-negative breast cancer trials, and outputs committed as open-access datasets. That is data creation with an experimental design attached — not scraping what exists, but funding the measurements that should exist, then giving them away. Two bids already failed this year and three datasets are held, two of them donated — so the foundation is learning in public that buying good data is harder than funding it.

A dormant benchmark wakes up

Enveda's CASMI 2026 runs September 14 to December 14 on Kaggle, reviving the Critical Assessment of Small Molecule Identification — founded in 2012, silent since 2022 — with about 400 unpublished molecules and 2,500 spectra as the blind test, 2.5M spectra for training including Enveda-180, and winning methods open-sourced as a persistent benchmark. The framing as the next AlphaFold contest is deliberate, and the underlying number justifies it: only about 10% of untargeted mass-spec signals are identifiable today.

The loop back into product is explicit: everything feeds PRISM, the Microsoft-built foundation model trained on over a billion spectra. Enveda pairs the open contest with pipeline proof — ENV-308 Phase 1 positive last month, ENV-294 at 85% mean reduction in Phase 1b atopic dermatitis earlier this year. Give away the benchmark, keep the engine it trains.

An RNA data factory opens its doors — halfway

Therna's Chronos crosses tens of thousands of synthetic mRNAs with 50 human cell lines over time, producing millions of functional RNA measurements per experiment where the convention is one mRNA, one cell type, one timepoint. Two datasets — Penta-47x27K and Tria-47x28K — are on Hugging Face with a white paper, aimed at sequence-to-function models for expression, stability, and cell-type specificity.

The reservation is as informative as the release: the immune-cell Chronos set stays proprietary. Open weights and open subsets for the community, closed data where the therapeutic value concentrates — the same "release the model, keep the measurements" equilibrium as the AISB Network pooling 20,167 proprietary structures to beat public-data models by double digits, except here one company plays both sides of the line itself.

Grounded infrastructure gets funded

Mithrl's $20M Series A, led by Obvious Ventures, sells the same diagnosis as a product: a biomedical world model under an agentic harness, forward-deployed inside client environments with no model lock-in, pitched against the hypothesis-generation flood with "reason from trusted studies, show receipts plus confidence." The claimed numbers — 16x more primary evidence per answer, 0.96 versus 0.60 scientific-correctness score, 45% fewer tokens, six-plus customer-owned patent filings — are vendor numbers from a launch announcement, not an independent benchmark, and should be read that way.

Still, the direction of travel is consistent across all four stories: the buyer is no longer asking for a smarter generator. They are asking for grounding — evidence attached, confidence stated, private data reachable without leaving the building. That is infrastructure language, not model language.

What to watch

Whether any of this compounds. A one-off $40M grant, a three-month contest, two open datasets, and a Series A are inputs, not outcomes. The tests are concrete: do the UNC open datasets get reused by groups that never touched cancer vaccines; does CASMI produce methods that survive contact with the 90% of unidentifiable spectra rather than the leaderboard; does Chronos train RNA models that design better sequences rather than merely describing old ones; does Mithrl's correctness score hold up inside a top-10 pharma's live portfolio. The field spent years learning that intelligence is widely shared and measurements are not. This week, for the first time, the money started acting like it.
