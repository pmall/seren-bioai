Protein to Pill

The best and worst month for rare gene therapy

28 September 2026

Two firsts and two failures, in the same month

Ultragenyx won FDA approval for Fayuvi, the first therapy for Sanfilippo syndrome type A, priced around $4M on the back of its Gene Transfer pivotal trial, alongside a push for newborn screening — and, weeks earlier, accelerated approval for Genglycos in glycogen storage disease type Ia, the company's first gene therapy to reach the market. Each approval came with a priority review voucher worth roughly $200M, with 2026 guidance of $730–760M excluding the new launches and $436M in cash at the end of June.

The same month delivered the other half of the ledger. The Phase 3 Aspire trial of the Angelman antisense oligonucleotide apazunersen missed on cognition, halved the stock, and was shelved for cost reasons, following January's setrusumab miss in osteogenesis imperfecta, where bone density improved but fracture rate did not, with an FDA path decision due by year end.

Why the pairing matters

Taken together, the month validates the rare-disease AAV platform technically while leaving the commercial question open. Two ultra-rare diseases with no prior options now have a genetic medicine — but at a $4M list price, with cost cuts already underway, the model only works if newborn screening finds the patients early enough and the voucher economics bridge the gap between development cost and a few dozen treatable children a year.

The antisense side sharpens into a split screen. The Angelman failure was a common-mutation, registrational-scale bet on cognition that did not move — while a physician with ultra-rare CHCHD10 mitochondrial ALS improved on a bespoke antisense after six intrathecal doses over a year, with neurofilament light normalized, motor scores improved or stable, and no serious adverse events, on a drug built in about three years versus a decade-plus for common-mutation oligos. One path is population-scale neurodevelopment measured in cognitive endpoints; the other is an n-of-1 measured in biomarkers and months of life. Both use the same delivery and chemistry stack, and right now they are telling opposite stories.

What to watch

Three things decide whether September reads as a turning point or a wash. First, whether Fayuvi and Genglycos convert approvals into treated patients — screening uptake, payer acceptance of the price, and how much of the two $200M vouchers is realized. Second, what happens to setrusumab's regulatory path by year end: a fracture-rate miss with a bone-density hit is exactly the kind of application where agency consistency matters. Third, which antisense lesson generalizes — whether the Angelman miss says cognition in neurodevelopmental disease needs different endpoints or different molecules, or whether the n-of-1 speed run says the future of neuro-oligos is smaller, faster, and personal.

If the gene therapies sell and the oligos narrow, the rare-disease playbook rewrites itself: AAV for the one-shot correction where the biology is crisp, bespoke oligos where it is unique, and nothing in between without a biomarker that moves early.
