Protein to Pill

The stake instead of the license

30 September 2026

In a single day, two pharmas paid more than $2 billion each for China-originated molecules — and structured the deals nothing alike. AstraZeneca paid $2 billion in stock for roughly 12% of Summit Therapeutics at $18.36 a share, without buying any commercial rights to ivonescimab. Novo Nordisk agreed to pay up to $2.6 billion, starting with $300 million upfront, for global rights to Hengrui's once-weekly oral peptide dual agonist HRS-1596. The day before, Merck had paid up to $2.13 billion ($400 million upfront, closed) for a preclinical oral KRAS G12D(ON) glue from SciBrunch. Bispecifics, incretin orals, KRAS glue: three modalities, one supply line.

What AstraZeneca actually bought

The Summit deal is equity, not a license. The $2 billion takes AstraZeneca to about 12% ownership and lifts Summit's cash to roughly $2.5 billion, funding global Phase 3 readouts for ivonescimab over the next two to four years. Summit keeps full economics in its territories — no commercial rights change hands.

What AstraZeneca gets alongside the stake is a combination, not a franchise: co-development of its CLDN18.2 antibody-drug conjugate sone-ve with ivonescimab in gastrointestinal cancers, with AstraZeneca sponsoring and costs shared. The molecule itself is Akeso-originated PD-1/VEGF bispecific ivonescimab — approved in China for second-line EGFR-mutant lung cancer in May 2024 and for first-line squamous disease last month, with mixed US readouts in HARMONi and HARMONi-3. The near-term tests are dated: a BLA decision on or before November 14, and overall-survival detail from HARMONi-GI1 in biliary cancer at ESMO next month. Paying $2 billion before either, and before commercial rights, is large pharma pricing pure exposure to the PD-1/VEGF class.

What Novo actually bought

The Hengrui deal is the conventional shape — global rights, upfront plus milestones — for an unconventional object: HRS-1596, a once-weekly oral peptide GLP-1R/GIPR dual agonist, Phase 1-ready and cleared in China for weight management and type 2 diabetes. Weekly oral plus dual agonism is long-term convenience differentiation, positioned behind Novo's CargiSema and cagrilintide programs — no near-term dynamic change, by the bank's own reading, but a different axis of competition if orals work.

Hengrui is on a run that makes the price legible: the Kailera $625 million deal and IPO, plus pacts with BMS, Merck, and GSK. The pattern is the same one behind the Merck KRAS glue and the ADC wave — Chinese assets rising from under 5% of deal value five years ago to more than two-thirds of 2026 deal value, from 35 deals worth $989 million in 2019 to 186 worth $137.7 billion in 2025. Incretins and bispecifics have now joined ADCs and KRAS on the import list.

Why the structure matters

Last week's bispecific deals were all licenses for discovery throughput — upfronts of $36.5 to $100 million with billion-plus tails for an option on someone else's engine. The Summit deal breaks that template: $2 billion committed, none of it for rights, all of it for balance-sheet runway plus a sponsored combination. It is a bet that the scarce thing is not the discovery platform but the Phase 3 survival of one specific bispecific, and that the way to own exposure is to keep the company alive rather than carve up the molecule.

That only makes sense if China-originated molecules are now first-line supply rather than a discount alternative — which the week's other half confirms. A preclinical glue with $65 million raised commands a $400 million upfront and a closed deal the same day its buyer withdraws an accelerated ADC filing for insufficient response data. Option money follows the originator, wherever it is incorporated.

What to watch

Whether the November 14 BLA decision and the ESMO survival detail validate the $2 billion of exposure before rights; whether HRS-1596 enters Phase 1 on schedule and what weekly-oral exposure actually looks like in humans; and whether the equity-without-license shape repeats — a second such stake would make it a structure, not an exception. Until then, the week's message is plain: the molecules come from China, and the paperwork is still being invented.
